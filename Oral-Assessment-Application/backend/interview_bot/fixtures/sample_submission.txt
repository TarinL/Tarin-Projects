Crime Prevention in New Zealand and Germany
Introduction
Crime prevention is a key challenge for governments worldwide, and comparing how different countries approach it can reveal useful insights for reform. This paper explores the crime prevention strategies of New Zealand and Germany, two developed nations with contrasting approaches to justice and rehabilitation. While both countries face rising public concern around issues like youth crime, gang activity, and social inequality, their criminal justice systems differ significantly in terms of incarceration rates, remand practices, and emphasis on rehabilitation. New Zealand tends to follow a more punitive path, with higher imprisonment rates and remand usage, especially among Māori. Germany, by contrast, focuses more on non-custodial sentencing and reintegration. This comparison aims to evaluate which methods have proven more effective and to suggest how each country could improve by learning from the other.
Economic Framework
From a public economics perspective, crime prevention represents a classic example of a public good; society as a whole benefits when crime is reduced, but private markets alone are unlikely to invest sufficiently in policing, justice, and rehabilitation. Following Becker’s (1968) model, crime can be viewed as a rational choice made by individuals weighing the expected benefits of offending against the expected costs, including the probability of apprehension and severity of punishment. The efficiency of crime prevention policies thus depends not only on deterrence but also on how governments allocate limited resources to reduce both the direct and indirect costs of crime.
New Zealand
A. Prevalence of Crime
In 2024, New Zealand had 223 offences per 10,000 people (Infometrics, 2025). The distribution of crime in New Zealand demonstrates significant regional variation, often corresponding to levels of deprivation, population density, and socio-economic inequality. Urban centres such as Rotorua, Napier, Hamilton, and Palmerston North report among the highest crime rates in the country, with over 50 offences per 10,000 people. (Stuff, 2020). In contrast, rural and affluent districts such as Selwyn and Central Otago consistently report some of the lowest rates of recorded crime, with monthly incidence rates under 15 per 10,000 people.
Demographically, Māori are substantially overrepresented in crime statistics. Although Māori constitute approximately 17% of the national population, they comprise over half of the prison population and account for roughly 75% of individuals listed on the national gang register (Perry, 2023). This disparity has been attributed to a range of structural factors, including intergenerational disadvantage, systemic bias in policing and judicial decision-making, and differential access to support services. Furthermore, younger males, particularly those aged between 15 and 25, remain disproportionately represented among both offenders and victims.
B. Policing
The New Zealand Police, as of late 2023, comprised approximately 10,211 sworn officers. The size of the Police force in New Zealand is a significant political issue. The Government has committed to recruiting an additional 500 frontline officers by the end of 2025. An additional $34.6 million was allocated for infrastructure and operational support, including fleet expansion and facility upgrades (New Zealand Government, 2023). However, the force continues to grapple with significant attrition, with a net outflow of officers to Australia driven by higher remuneration and recruitment incentives. This has raised concerns about staffing shortfalls, particularly in smaller centres (Schwarten, 2024).
New Zealand’s policing approach since 2011 has been guided by the Prevention First strategy, which reoriented the organisation towards proactive, community-centred service delivery. Officers continue to perform traditional functions such as crime detection, traffic enforcement, and public order maintenance but there is greater emphasis on preventing crime before it occurs through partnership-based initiatives. While the model is intended to reduce victimisation and the demand for reactive services, there has been criticism that it has also overextended frontline officers, who are now expected to respond to complex social issues such as mental health crises and family harm (den Heyer, 2021).
In 2023/2024, total expenditure on police services was approximately $2.72 billion. This funding supported operational capability, technology upgrades, crime prevention initiatives, and partnerships with local authorities and iwi. Despite these investments, there remains ongoing public scrutiny of police responsiveness, particularly in areas experiencing elevated crime or gang activity.
C. Criminal Justice System
New Zealand’s criminal justice system continues to reflect a punitive orientation, with high rates of remand and incarceration relative to other OECD jurisdictions. As of December 2024, 41.7% of the male prison population and 56.2% of the female prison population were held on remand (Department of Corrections, 2025). The remand system has been criticised for breaching international human rights norms and imposing high fiscal and social costs, particularly given the elevated rates of remand among Māori and the lack of access to rehabilitation programmes for remand detainees (Khushal, 2023).
Although New Zealand’s overall crime rate, when measured per capita, is broadly in line with other developed nations, certain categories of offending appear to be elevated. The country’s incarceration rate, currently at 187 prisoners per 100,000 population, is among the highest in the OECD. This contrasts sharply with other countries, suggesting that while the volume of recorded offences may not be markedly higher, punitive criminal justice policies may drive up imprisonment levels (Perry, 2023). Additionally, New Zealand has experienced high rates of certain violent offences and youth offending relative to peer countries, with particular concern directed at the rise in youth-led ram raids and gang-related violence.
Recidivism remains a persistent issue. Within two years of release, approximately 56.5% of former prisoners are reconvicted, and 35.8% are reimprisoned. These figures suggest that current rehabilitative measures may be insufficient in addressing the underlying causes of offending or in supporting effective reintegration (Department of Corrections, 2025).
Timeliness in New Zealand’s criminal justice system has also deteriorated significantly, particularly since the Covid-19 pandemic. Criminal trials now regularly extend two to three years from charging to disposition, undermining procedural efficiency and the deterrence value of legal sanctions (Stuff, 2024). Easton (Economides, 2013) identifies a range of “invisible determinants” behind disposition time, including judicial capacity, listing practices, and courtroom scheduling. It has been acknowledged that current initiatives are unlikely to reverse trends without structural investment in judicial resources and system-wide coordination (Ministry of Justice, 2023).
D. Private Crime Prevention
In addition to state-led interventions, private and community-based efforts contribute significantly to crime prevention in New Zealand. Organisations such as Neighbourhood Support New Zealand work in collaboration with police and local councils to encourage vigilance, reporting, and community cohesion. With over 200,000 households engaged nationwide, these programmes promote crime deterrence through informal surveillance, information sharing, and emergency preparedness (Neighbourhood Support, 2022).
Private security firms also represent a growing component of the crime prevention landscape. The sector has expanded in response to commercial demand, with businesses, schools, and public transport providers increasingly investing in CCTV surveillance, static guards, and alarm systems. While these services can deter opportunistic offending, questions remain regarding their regulation, accountability, and overall impact on crime rates.
E. Strengths and Weaknesses of Crime Prevention in New Zealand
New Zealand’s crime prevention framework benefits from strong community-police partnerships and a growing focus on evidence-based approaches. Initiatives such as the Evidence-Based Policing Centre have strengthened the use of data to inform deployment and strategy. However, systemic weaknesses persist. The country’s high incarceration and remand rates, persistent recidivism, and the disproportionate criminalisation of Māori point to structural inequities. Moreover, staffing pressures within the police and courts risk undermining the efficacy of frontline crime prevention efforts.
Germany
A. Prevalence of Crime
Recently, Germany's crime rate has increased, reaching 5.6 million offences in 2022, around 6762 crimes per 100,000 people, the first increase in the last seven years. Both violent crime and cybercrime are on the rise, with ~5% increases year on year, though homicide rates remain low, around 0.9 per 100,000, making Germany one of the safer OECD nations. There are pronounced regional and demographic differences. Urbanised and city states have far higher crime rates than rural areas. For example, Berlin saw ~520,000 crimes in 2022, translating to roughly double the national per capita rate. Other major cities like Hamburg, Bremen, and Frankfurt also report above-average crime incidence. More rural states, such as Bavaria, have comparatively lower crime rates. With cities, lower-income districts tend to experience more crime, and youth crime has surged post-pandemic.
Foreign nationals are statistically overrepresented as suspects and offenders. In 2023, 34.4% of crime suspects did not hold German citizenship (excluding immigration offences), despite foreign residents only making up 16.5% of the population. A subset of particular focus is asylum seekers, who comprised 11.1% of all suspects in 2022, despite being a much smaller share of the population. It is important to note that these are suspects, not convictions, and may be influenced by potentially higher reporting rates when suspects are foreigners.
B. Policing
As of 2023, Germany had around 260,000 sworn police officers across its 16 federal states, each managing its own force within a decentralised system. National agencies like the Federal Criminal Police Office (BKA) and Federal Police focus on counterterrorism, cybercrime, and border security. While overall staffing remains stable, recruitment and retention are growing concerns, especially for specialised roles in digital crime and public order.
Combined federal and state spending on policing and internal security exceeded €30 billion in 2023, funding operations, infrastructure, and prevention efforts. However, regional disparities remain, and some urban areas face challenges filling specialised positions.
C. Criminal Justice 
Germany's criminal justice system emphasises legal rigour and rehabilitation. Incarceration rates are low by international standards, with 56,300 people incarcerated in 2022, around 67 inmates per 100,000. Prison populations have declined over the past decade, while crime has fluctuated. The number of pre-trial detainees is increasing as courts face backlogs. Foreign nationals form a significant share of inmates, around 40% of prisoners as of 2024, possibly driven by the fact that non-citizens convicted in Germany are less easily able to access alternatives to imprisonment, such as probation.
Sentencing focuses on rehabilitation; most convictions result in fines or suspended sentences, while imprisonment and long terms, especially, are reserved for more serious crimes. Despite this, Germany still has relatively high recidivism rates, around 46% of released prisoners are convicted of a new offence within three years. However, only one-third of recidivists receive another prison sentence without probation, so only 15% of inmates end up back in prison within three years of release. Policies like open prisons are used to facilitate reintegration, and studies show inmates in open conditions reoffend less frequently.
The court system is generally efficient, but under strain from rising case loads. Prosecutors handled 5.5 million cases in 2023, an increase of 8% compared to the previous year. Most cases are resolved relatively quickly, however, complex cases can take years. Judicial delays have become a concern, with 933000 cases pending in late 2024.
Recommendation for Germany
Recommendation: Adopt culturally responsive policing similar to New Zealand’s Māori Wardens.
Culturally responsive policing in New Zealand has emerged as a crucial strategy to address the historical and ongoing marginalisation of Māori within the criminal justice system. One of the most longstanding and prominent examples is the Māori Warden system, established under the Māori Community Development Act 1962. Māori Wardens are community-based volunteers who support public safety and social cohesion through culturally appropriate methods such as de-escalation, mediation, and support for at-risk youth and intoxicated individuals (Te Puni Kōkiri, n.d.). They are often used as a mechanism to divert young offenders out of the court system (Butterworth, 2005). Unlike conventional policing, Wardens are not armed and do not possess formal enforcement powers, but they are highly effective in building trust within Māori communities. Their success lies in their legitimacy, derived from shared whakapapa, language, and cultural knowledge, which economic models of crime suggest are crucial in increasing voluntary compliance with legal norms (Tyler, 2006).
The effectiveness of Māori Wardens reflects a government approach to crime prevention that engages with a different aspect of a criminal’s utility analysis, rather than punishment. Māori Wardens are cost-effective alternatives to formal policing, particularly in areas where distrust of state institutions is high. Wardens can respond to situations where there is a risk of criminal behaviour and leverage their social capital and cultural familiarity to intervene in a way that promotes social norms. Through reference to shared social expectations, a Warden’s intervention can increase the psychic cost of criminal behaviour, which may have been minimised by low social trust, cultural differences or drug/alcohol abuse. Further, the Warden’s legitimacy they foster may reduce recidivism by promoting long-term behavioural change rather than relying solely on punitive deterrence. 
Germany, in contrast, faces increasing demands for more inclusive approaches to public safety, particularly in its growing migrant and refugee communities. These groups often experience institutional alienation, underreporting of crime, and social exclusion exacerbated by a police force that is not competent at intercultural conflict resolution (Vogt & Kersten, 2015). A culturally responsive policing framework modelled on the Māori Warden system could offer an innovative solution. Local “Migrant Safety Liaisons,” embedded within Turkish, Arab, or African communities, could function in similar ways: supporting police during community patrols, resolving low-level disputes, and mediating intergenerational or intercultural tensions. Importantly, these liaisons would require legal frameworks to ensure cultural sensitivity without reinforcing segregation or parallel systems of law.
Recommendation for New Zealand
Recommendation: Reduce the prison population by decreasing pre-trial detention and short-term imprisonment.
New Zealand's criminal justice system is characterised by a punitive orientation, evidenced by its high rates of remand and incarceration. This reliance on pre-trial detention incurs substantial financial and social costs, particularly for Maori who are disproportionately affected, and can increase the likelihood of re-offending by exposing individuals to prison culture without access to rehabilitation. In contrast, Germany’s criminal justice system prioritises legal rigour and rehabilitation, resulting in considerably lower incarceration. While they do also face challenges with increases in pre-trial detainees, their approach emphasises non-custodial sentences, such as fines and suspended sentences, reserving imprisonment for more serious crimes. A smaller portion of released prisoners (15%) return to prison within three years, suggesting these techniques may be effective. Other policies, like open prisons, also help facilitate reintegration.
Drawing lessons from Germany’s rehabilitative emphasis, New Zealand could implement a strategy aimed at reducing its reliance on pre-trial detention and short-term imprisonment, thereby mitigating associated costs and improving reintegration outcomes. This intervention would focus on prioritising non-custodial sentences for minor offences and investing in targeted rehabilitation pathways. Emulating Germany’s sentencing philosophy, New Zealand courts should be encouraged to utilise non-custodial sentencing, such as fines, community work, and suspended sentences, more often for non-violent or minor offences. This would reduce the prison population, freeing up correctional resources, and minimising the negative consequences of short-term imprisonment, which often disrupts employment, housing, and family life without significant rehabilitative benefit. Another important component of reducing recidivism is effective rehabilitation. Drawing inspiration from Germany’s use of open prisons, New Zealand could expand its use of transitional facilities and structured rehabilitation programmes, providing a gradual return to community life and offering support for housing, employment, and social connections post-release. This proactive approach aims to increase the psychic cost of offending by strengthening individuals' legitimate pathways to success and reducing the perceived utility of criminal behaviour.
The economic rationale for these interventions is compelling. Reducing the number of individuals held on remand, as well as decreasing the overall incarceration rate, would generate significant savings, which could be reallocated to more effective prevention and rehabilitation initiatives. Through reduced recidivism, these strategies would reduce the long-term social and economic costs associated with crime. Furthermore, by fostering reintegration, these policies contribute to a more productive workforce and stronger communities.
